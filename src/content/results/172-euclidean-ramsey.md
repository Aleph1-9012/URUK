Colour every point of space with finitely many colours, as badly as you like. Some shapes cannot be avoided: in a high enough dimension, any colouring contains two points at distance one of the same colour, and in fact a whole equilateral triangle, a whole regular simplex. Which finite shapes have this property? Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer and Straus founded Euclidean Ramsey theory in 1973 and proved a necessary condition: the shape must lie on a sphere. Graham conjectured that lying on a sphere is also sufficient; Leader, Russell and Walters proposed in 2012 a rival answer, that a shape is Ramsey exactly when it sits inside a finite set with a transitive symmetry group. Frankl and Rödl proved all simplices Ramsey, Kříž all sets with soluble transitive symmetry, Cantwell the regular polytopes; but neither conjecture could be settled, and the two disagree about simple shapes, such as some cyclic quadrilaterals. In September 2026 both moved at once: Pálvölgyi posted a seven-point configuration on a circle that is not Ramsey, refuting Graham's conjecture in a manuscript he says was written by ChatGPT, and on 23 September OpenAI posted a paper claiming a complete algebraic classification, from which it follows that some cyclic quadrilaterals are Ramsey without being subtransitive, refuting the other conjecture too. [1][2][3]

As of 20 October 2026 no mathematician has said publicly that they have read or checked the proof. The status on this page is "unreviewed".

## The problem

A finite nonempty set $A$ in Euclidean space is Ramsey if for every $r\ge2$ there is a dimension $D$ such that every map $c:\mathbb R^{D}\to\{1,\dots,r\}$, with no measurability or regularity assumed, is constant on some congruent copy of $A$, at the original scale. [1]

**The classification problem.** Characterize the finite Ramsey sets up to congruence. Graham's conjecture: Ramsey if and only if spherical. Leader, Russell and Walters' Conjecture A: Ramsey if and only if subtransitive, that is, isometric to a subset of a finite set on which its isometry group acts transitively. [1][2]

## Why it stayed open

What was known. Ramsey implies spherical (Erdős et al.). Nondegenerate simplices are Ramsey (Frankl and Rödl), as are finite sets with soluble transitive symmetry and cyclic trapezoids (Kříž), vertex sets of regular polytopes (Cantwell), and the result of adding a point outside the affine span of a Ramsey set (Moore; Mirabi). Leader, Russell and Walters exhibited cyclic kites that are not subtransitive and conjectured they are not Ramsey. Pálvölgyi's heptagon of September 2026 shows that spherical is not enough. [1]

Why it was hard. Arbitrary colourings have no continuity, so an approximate copy of a shape is useless; the proofs of the positive results went through Hales-Jewett-type theorems on words and products, which are available only when the shape has enough symmetry. A criterion for an arbitrary shape had to be algebraic, and no one knew what algebra. [1]

## What OpenAI claims

**Theorem** (Classification; OpenAI, 23 September 2026). Let $A=\{a_1,\dots,a_s\}\subset\mathbb R^{d}$ be a finite set of at least two points with affine span $\mathbb R^{d}$. Let $p_i=(1,a_i)^{T}$, let $F$ be the field generated over $\mathbb Q$ by all coordinates of all $a_i$, let $B=F\otimes_{\mathbb Q}F$ with multiplication map $m_F(x\otimes y)=xy$. Then $A$ is Ramsey if and only if there is a matrix $P\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d+1}(B)$ with

$$
(p_i\otimes1)^{T}P\,(1\otimes p_i)=0\quad(1\le i\le s),\qquad m_F(P_{\alpha\beta})=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\quad(1\le\alpha,\beta\le d).
$$

[1]

**Consequences.** Applying $m_F$ to the first equation gives an ordinary sphere equation, so Ramsey implies spherical; the tensor condition asks the identity to hold before multiplication, and that is the gap between spherical and Ramsey. Every nonempty subtransitive set satisfies the condition, so the sufficiency half of Leader, Russell and Walters' conjecture holds. Every nonempty set of at most five points on a circle is Ramsey, hence every cyclic quadrilateral, including their non-subtransitive kites, which refutes the necessity half and their Conjecture 3. Nine circle points with algebraically independent parameters are not Ramsey, and a twelve-point set of three rotated squares has a fifty-colour obstruction in every dimension. [1]

What this does and does not say. The criterion uses exact relations in the coordinate field; the paper says it "is not a procedure for recovering those relations from numerical approximations to arbitrary real coordinates". Singletons and the affine-span reduction are handled separately. [1]

## How the claimed proof goes

What follows is a map of the paper's argument from its introduction, which sets out the three stages, and from the main statements of Sections 2 to 7. It is not a check of the details. [1]

**The criterion.** For $A=\{a_1,\dots,a_s\}\subset\mathbb R^{d}$ with affine span $\mathbb R^{d}$, let $p_i=(1,a_i)$, let $F$ be the field generated over $\mathbb Q$ by the coordinates, and $B=F\otimes_{\mathbb Q}F$ with its multiplication map $m_F(x\otimes y)=xy$. Theorem 1.1: $A$ is Ramsey if and only if there is a $(d+1)\times(d+1)$ matrix $P$ over $B$ with $(p_i\otimes1)^{T}P(1\otimes p_i)=0$ for every $i$ and $m_F(P_{\alpha\beta})=\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ for $1\le\alpha,\beta\le d$. Applying $m_F$ to the evaluation equations gives an ordinary sphere equation $\|a_i\|^{2}+\ell\cdot a_i+c=0$, so every Ramsey set is spherical, as Erdős and his coauthors knew; the tensor condition demands that the identities hold before multiplication, and that is exactly the gap between sphericity and the Ramsey property. The condition uses exact relations in the coordinate field and is not a numerical procedure. [1]

**Necessity (Section 2).** The matrix is replaced by a finite rational combination of tensors of affine functions on $\mathbb R^{d}$ whose evaluations at the points of $A$ vanish as rational tensors and whose gradient Gram matrix is the identity (Proposition 2.1, the equivalence). Proposition 2.4: if no such tensor exists, consider the $\mathbb Q$-linear map $\Phi$ from tensors to $(\mathbb R\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb R)^{s}\oplus\mathrm{Sym}_d(\mathbb R)$ sending $T$ to its evaluations and its Gram matrix; the target point $(0,\dots,0,I_d)$ lies outside the image, so a $\mathbb Q$-linear functional separates it, and from the functional one builds a finite colouring of $\mathbb R^{D}$ for every $D$ with no monochromatic congruent copy of $A$. This is the algebraic form of the classical sphericity obstruction. [1]

**Sufficiency, stage one: exact copies at two scales (Sections 3 and 4).** The difficulty, the paper says, is that arbitrary colourings provide no continuity with which to absorb approximation errors, so every cancellation must be exact. The tensor determines signed rational multiplicities for a finite list of affine functions, with positive and negative multiplicities cancelling separately at each evaluation value at every point of $A$ (Lemma 3.1, a finite lattice identity). A Gaussian construction, followed by discretization and rational approximation within a fixed convolution image (Lemma 4.2, a compactly supported smoothing function; Lemma 4.3, finite rational weights), makes the negative gradient second moment small while keeping the cancellations exact. Evaluating the two lists at the points gives vectors with

$$
\|f_i-f_j\|^{2}=\|a_i-a_j\|^{2},\qquad\|g_i-g_j\|^{2}=q\|a_i-a_j\|^{2},
$$

for arbitrarily small $q>0$, each $g_i$ a coordinate permutation of $f_i$ (Lemma 4.1, two-scale configurations). Both the distances and the matching coordinate multisets are exact. [1]

**Stage two: paths in a free group (Section 5).** A free-group generator is attached to each finitely supported real sequence. A path is an ordered product of $s$-tuples of group elements, each factor either recording an ordered scaled copy of $A$ or having the same element in every position; its endpoint is the coordinatewise product and its weight the sum of the squared copy scales. Proposition 5.1, the path criterion: equal endpoints with arbitrarily small weight ratios force the Ramsey property. The obstacle, familiar from Leader, Russell and Walters' group and word methods, is that the number $t$ of variable positions in a Hales-Jewett line is not known in advance. The paper fixes the Hales-Jewett word length $n$ and prepares one endpoint at weights $1,1/2,\dots,1/n$ before colouring the words (Lemma 5.3, synchronization); an ultrafilter construction realizes the products by finite strings while keeping common factors identical (Lemma 5.5, simultaneous realization), the $t$ variable positions contribute a squared-distance multiplier $t\cdot(1/t)=1$, and compactness (Lemma 5.2) supplies a finite ambient dimension for each number of colours. [1]

**Stage three: equal endpoints (Section 6).** The permutation pairs from stage one must be made to have equal path endpoints. Permutation averaging puts the discrepancies between their group entries in a subgroup whose elements can be corrected by appending paths of unit-scale copies (Lemmas 6.1 to 6.3), and further averaging makes the total correction weight small relative to the larger original weight. Appending the corrections produces the equal endpoints and arbitrarily small weight ratios the path criterion requires (Proposition 6.4), which proves sufficiency. All arguments are in ZFC. [1]

**Consequences (Section 7).** Every subtransitive set satisfies the tensor condition (Corollary 7.2), recovering the Leader-Russell-Walters theorem. A second sufficient condition is that evaluation at the points of a spherical set gives independent functionals on quadratic polynomials (Proposition 7.3); it applies to every set of at most five points on a circle, hence to every cyclic quadrilateral (Corollary 7.4), and combined with Leader, Russell and Walters' non-subtransitive cyclic kites it shows those kites are Ramsey (Corollary 7.5), refuting the necessity direction of their proposed characterization. On the negative side, nine points from algebraically independent parameters on a circle fail the tensor condition by a determinant calculation (Proposition 7.6), a conclusion already implied by Pálvölgyi's generic heptagon, and a twelve-point configuration of three rotated squares has a tensor obstruction built from weighted derivation moments of the kind used in his construction (Proposition 7.7). [1][2]

Where would an expert look first? At the separation argument of Proposition 2.4; at the exactness of the cancellations after rational approximation in Section 4, since the paper itself says no approximation error can be absorbed; at the weight preparation of Lemma 5.3 that circumvents the unknown $t$; and at the kites corollary, which is short and can be checked against Leader, Russell and Walters' paper. [1][2]

## Consequences inside mathematics

If the proof holds, the fifty-year classification problem of Euclidean Ramsey theory has an exact answer, both standing conjectures are false in one direction each, Graham's by Pálvölgyi's heptagon and Leader-Russell-Walters' by the cyclic kites, while the other direction of the latter becomes a theorem; and every earlier positive result is a special case of the tensor criterion. Whether the criterion can be decided for shapes given numerically is a new question. [1][2]

## Uses outside mathematics

No known direct application.

Euclidean Ramsey theory is pure combinatorial geometry; its nearest relatives in applications are questions about unavoidable patterns in point configurations, but the theorem concerns arbitrary colourings of high-dimensional space and has no engineering counterpart. [1]
