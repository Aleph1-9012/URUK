Take $n$ switches and a list of rules, each saying "at least one of these three switches must be in the position I name". Is there a setting of the switches that obeys every rule? This is 3-SAT, the problem at the centre of the theory of computation since 1971. Nobody knows a method for it that is fundamentally faster than trying the settings one after another, and in 1999 Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi proposed that none exists: any method must take time that grows like a fixed power of two per switch. That proposal, the Exponential Time Hypothesis, became one of the load-bearing assumptions of computer science. On 26 October 2026 OpenAI published a paper, dated 17 October and revised 25 October, claiming an algorithm that beats it, and a Lean formalization of a slightly weaker form of the claim. Hours later Anthropic published an independent claim of the same statement, credited to one of its own models. [1][2][11][19]

As of 1 November 2026 the people examining the proof are the hypothesis's own authors, Impagliazzo and Paturi, together with Ryan Williams of MIT, who have said publicly that their involvement "should not be taken as certification of the claim". Several theorists have written expositions, each with a disclaimer. No named expert has yet said in public that the proof holds, and none has reported an error. The status on this page is "under review". [12][15][16]

## The problem

A Boolean variable is a switch with two positions, true and false. A literal is a variable or its negation. A clause is a list of literals joined by "or"; it is satisfied when at least one of them is true. A formula in conjunctive normal form (a CNF) is a list of clauses joined by "and", and a $k$-CNF is one whose clauses have at most $k$ literals each. The formula

$$
(x_1\vee x_2\vee x_3)\wedge(\neg x_1\vee\neg x_2\vee\neg x_3)
$$

has three variables, two clauses, and six satisfying assignments out of the eight possible, because only "all true" and "all false" break a rule. $k$-SAT asks whether a given $k$-CNF has at least one satisfying assignment; $\#k$-SAT asks how many it has. [1]

For $k=2$ there is a fast method, linear in the size of the formula. For $k=3$ the question changes character. Cook proved in 1971 that 3-SAT is NP-complete: every problem whose solutions can be checked quickly can be rewritten as a 3-SAT instance, so a fast general method for 3-SAT would be a fast method for all of them. That is the question P versus NP, and almost everyone in the field believes the answer is no. [4]

Exhaustive search tries all $2^{n}$ settings. Cleverer methods do better, but only by lowering the base of the exponential. Schöning's 1999 algorithm, which starts from a random setting and repeatedly flips a variable in a broken rule, decides 3-SAT in about $(4/3)^{n}$ steps. The PPSZ algorithm of Paturi, Pudlák, Saks and Zane brought the base down to about $1.308$, and the best deterministic bound before this paper was about $1.328^{n}$. Each of these is still of the form $c^{n}$ for some $c>1$: doubling the number of variables squares the running time. [1][5][6]

The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) says this cannot be avoided. In its standard form: there is a constant $\delta>0$ such that no algorithm decides 3-SAT in time $2^{\delta n}$ on every instance. Equivalently, the base of the exponential cannot be pushed arbitrarily close to $1$. An algorithm with running time $2^{o(n)}$, meaning $2^{\epsilon n}$ for every $\epsilon>0$ once $n$ is large, is called subexponential; ETH says no such algorithm exists. The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) says more: as the clause width $k$ grows, the best possible base for $k$-SAT tends to $2$, the base of exhaustive search. [1][2][3]

## Why it stayed open

### Who asked

Impagliazzo and Paturi stated the hypothesis in a 1999 conference paper, published in 2001 as "On the complexity of $k$-SAT". They defined $s_k$ as the smallest exponent achievable for $k$-SAT, showed that the sequence $s_3\le s_4\le\cdots$ increases infinitely often if it is positive at all, and proposed both that $s_3>0$ (ETH) and that the limit is $1$ (what was later named SETH). With Francis Zane, in "Which problems have strongly exponential complexity?", they proved the sparsification lemma: a $k$-CNF can be split into $2^{\epsilon n}$ formulas with only linearly many clauses whose solutions partition the original's. The lemma makes ETH for 3-SAT equivalent to ETH for sparse 3-SAT, and from there to subexponential-time equivalence for a whole class of problems: $k$-colourability, independent set, vertex cover, clique, and others. Fail for one and you fail for all. [2][3]

### What was tried

Thirty years of algorithm design produced a sequence of better bases and no subexponential algorithm. Schöning's local search, PPSZ and its biased refinements by Hansen, Kaplan, Zamir and Zwick, Scheder's sharper analysis of the original PPSZ, Liu's deterministic covering-code algorithm: each improved the base for small $k$ by amounts that shrank as $k$ grew, and that shrinking was itself the main evidence for SETH. Exact counting was harder still: the best $\#3$-SAT algorithms ran in about $1.637^{n}$, and the counting version of the hypothesis, $\#$ETH, was formulated in 2014 to underpin lower bounds for the permanent and the Tutte polynomial. [1][6][10]

On the other side, the hypotheses became tools. Once ETH is assumed, reductions that blow up the instance by a controlled amount give lower bounds for other problems: no $f(k)\,n^{o(k)}$ algorithm for finding a $k$-clique, no $2^{o(\sqrt m)}$ algorithm for clique on $m$ edges, tight bounds for algorithms on graphs of bounded treewidth. SETH, through the orthogonal vectors problem, gave quadratic lower bounds for edit distance, longest common subsequence and dynamic time warping, among the most cited results of the 2010s. Hundreds of papers rest on one of the two hypotheses. [1][7][15]

### What stood in the way

Every known approach to $k$-SAT works locally: it branches on variables, follows implications, or walks between neighbouring assignments. Such methods see the formula one clause at a time, and a formula whose clauses tie every variable to every other one gives them no foothold short of enumerating most assignments. Algebraic methods, which rewrite a Boolean problem as a question about polynomials, were known to give speedups of a different kind: Williams showed in 2016 that a Merlin-Arthur protocol can certify unsatisfiability in about $2^{n/2}$ time, refuting the Merlin-Arthur version of SETH, and his 2010 work connected any improvement over exhaustive search for circuit satisfiability to circuit lower bounds, a connection that produced the first lower bounds against $\mathrm{ACC}$ circuits. But these savings were of the form $2^{n-n^{\epsilon}}$ or needed a prover; nothing suggested that polynomials could cut the exponent itself. As Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams wrote on 26 October, "ETH has helped organize decades of research, connecting problems and sharpening our understanding of computational difficulty." [1][8][9][12]

## What OpenAI claims

Write a $k$-CNF on $n$ variables with $m$ clauses, and write $\#F$ for the number of its satisfying assignments. Throughout, $\log$ is the natural logarithm, and $O_k(\cdot)$ hides a constant depending only on $k$. [1]

**Theorem 1.1** (OpenAI, 25 October 2026). For each fixed $k\ge2$, there is a deterministic algorithm that, given a $k$-CNF $F$ on $n\ge20$ variables with $m$ clauses, computes $\#F$ in

$$
(n+m)^{O_k(1)}\,2^{O_k\left(n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n}\right)}
$$

bit operations and $(n+m)^{O_k(1)}$ bits of space. If moreover $m\le Dn$ for a fixed $D>0$, the running time is $(n+m)^{O_{k,D}(1)}2^{O_{k,D}(n/\sqrt{\log n})}$. [1]

Since $\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n}\to0$, the exponent is $o(n)$: for every $\delta>0$ the algorithm runs in $2^{\delta n}$ time once $n$ is large enough. Testing whether $\#F>0$ decides satisfiability, so the theorem refutes ETH, and hence SETH. The paper also lists the gap version (Gap-ETH), the nondeterministic version (NSETH), the counting version ($\#$ETH) and the bounded-error randomized versions as refuted, because the algorithm is deterministic and exact. [1]

Three corollaries follow in the paper. Corollary 1.2: for every $\epsilon>0$, a randomized algorithm finds an assignment within $\epsilon m$ of the maximum number of satisfiable clauses in $2^{o(n)}$ time. Corollary 1.3: the number of independent sets, of proper $q$-colourings and of perfect matchings of a graph can be counted in time $2^{o(v)}$, respectively $2^{o(v+e)}$, where $v$ and $e$ count vertices and edges. Corollary 1.4: there is a language in $\mathrm E^{\mathrm{NP}}$, the class decided in time $2^{O(n)}$ with an NP oracle, that needs more than $c\,n\sqrt{\log n}$ gates in any fan-in-two circuit, for infinitely many $n$. [1]

Four points of scope matter.

The clause width $k$ is fixed before the input. The paper is explicit that $2^{o(n)}$ bounds "do not transfer under general polynomial-time reductions", because a reduction may multiply the number of variables; the result "does not imply that general NP problems admit subexponential time algorithms". Subset Sum and the travelling salesman problem gain nothing from it. It says nothing about P versus NP. [1][15]

The constants are not explicit. The theorem asserts the existence of $O_k(1)$ and $O_k(\cdot)$ constants, and the proof chooses parameters "for sufficiently large $n$", handling smaller inputs by exhaustive search. We solved the paper's own parameter constraints from its technique overview (its Section 2.8, Step 5): the three inequalities it checks, $r\ge2$, $R\ge32$ and $6R\le Q$, are first satisfied when $\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ is about $1.09\times10^{10}$, that is for $n$ beyond $2^{10^{10}}$, a number with about $3.3$ billion decimal digits. The paper does not state this figure and makes no claim about practical speed. [1]

Two bounds appear. The paper's text proves the exponent $n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n}$, and $n/\sqrt{\log n}$ for linear-size formulas, through a tensor-contraction argument in Appendix D. Its rational-interpolation route (Section 6) gives only $n(\log\log n)^{2}/\sqrt{\log n}$, and that weaker exponent is the one in the Lean formalization. All three are $o(n)$, so the refutation does not depend on which is right. [1][22]

The release is unusual in form. OpenAI says its model found the natural-language proof on 16 October and the Lean proof was completed on 17 October; the manuscript is dated 17 October, revised 25 October, 67 pages. A second OpenAI manuscript of 17 pages, "Exactly counting 3-SAT solutions in subexponential time", with a weaker exponent, sits in the examiners' shared folder and is not in the GitHub repository. [1][11][23]

## How the claimed proof goes

What follows is a map of the argument, built from the paper's technique overview (Section 2) and the sections it points to. It is not a check of the details. The paper is in six sections and four appendices; Section 2 proves the result for 3-CNFs in which every variable occurs at most three times, and the rest of the paper removes that restriction and sharpens the exponent. [1]

**The count as a polynomial (Section 2.2).** Each clause $e$ becomes a polynomial $\Phi_e$ that is $1$ on satisfying inputs and $0$ otherwise: for $x_a\vee\neg x_b\vee x_c$ it is $1-(1-X_a)X_b(1-X_c)$. Then $\#F=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\prod_e\Phi_e(x)$. With at most three occurrences per variable the product has degree at most $3n$. This is the only use of the occurrence bound: a linear degree. [1]

**The wish (Section 2.2).** If the variables could be split into $r$ groups of size at most $2n/r$ with every clause inside one group, the count would be a product of $r$ small counts, each found by exhaustive search in $2^{2n/r}$ steps. Real formulas do not split this way. The whole proof is a device for making them split algebraically. [1]

**Deformation (Section 2.3).** Introduce a parameter $T$. In each clause, replace every occurrence of a variable $X_v$ by a linear form $\langle\vec P_{e,v}(T),\vec X\rangle$ whose coefficients are polynomials in $T$ of degree at most $R$, with $\vec P_{e,v}(0)=\vec e_v$ so that $T=0$ restores the original variable. The deformed count $H(T)=\sum_x\prod_e\Phi_e(\langle\vec P_{e,v}(T),x\rangle)$ is a polynomial with $H(0)=\#F$ and $\deg H\le3nR$. At $T=0$ nothing has changed; the point is to choose the forms so that at $T=1,\ldots,Q$ the sum becomes cheap. [1]

**The design (Section 2.4, Definition 2.1).** At a point $t$, an $(r,\tau,D)$-design consists of a partition of the variables into $r$ groups of size at most $2n/r$, an assignment of each clause to one group, and for each group an integer matrix $C_g$ of "measurements", at most $2\tau n$ rows in all, each with coefficients summing in absolute value to at most $D$. The condition is that every replacement form of a clause assigned to group $g$ depends on the other groups only through their measurements $y_h=C_hx_h$. Once the measurement tuple $\vec y$ is fixed, every clause polynomial depends on its own group alone, and

$$
H(t)=\sum_{\vec y}\prod_{g=1}^{r}\Big(\sum_{u:\,C_gu=y_g}A_{t,g}(u;\vec y)\Big).
$$

The algorithm enumerates the at most $(2D+1)^{2\tau n}$ measurement tuples and, for each, the at most $r\,2^{2n/r}$ assignments to single groups, keeping only running sums (Lemma 3.2 and Proposition 3.4 give the general routine over any commutative $\mathbb Q$-algebra). [1]

**Taylor recovery (Section 2.5).** $Q$ values do not determine a polynomial of degree $3nR$, so the algorithm computes $J=1+\lfloor3nR/Q\rfloor$ Taylor coefficients of $H$ at each point $t$; the difference of two candidates would be divisible by $\prod_t(T-t)^{J}$, of degree exceeding $3nR$, so the data determine $H$ and the constant term is $\#F$. The obstacle is that the design controls the forms at $t$, not their derivatives. The paper separates the shift: it writes the forms at $t+z$ as the forms at $t$ plus a correction $\langle\Delta\vec P(z),\vec X\rangle$ with a separate formal input $\vec X$, notes that every term of order $z^{j}$ involves at most $j$ coordinates of $\vec X$, and recovers the sum with $\vec X=x$ by inclusion-exclusion over subsets $U\subseteq E$ of size at most $q=J-1$ from sums with $\vec X$ frozen at indicator vectors $\mathbf 1_U$ (Lemma 3.3, the Boolean-difference identity). Each frozen sum is a design sum, computed in the truncated ring $\mathbb Q[z]/(z^{J})$. [1]

**Three costs (Section 2.6).** The total work is, up to polynomial factors, $\sum_{j\le q}2^{j}\binom nj$ for the Taylor data, times $(2D+1)^{2\tau n}$ for the measurements, times $2^{2n/r}$ for one group. Its logarithm is $O\big(n[\tfrac RQ\log\tfrac{2Q}{R}+\tau\log(2D+1)+\tfrac1r]\big)$. The parameters that make all three terms small are

$$
r=\Theta\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\Big),\quad Q=\Theta\Big(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\Big),\quad R=\Theta(\sqrt{\log n}),\quad \tau=\Theta\Big(\frac1{r\log n}\Big),\quad D=r^{2Q}\le(2n)^{1/128},
$$

which give the exponent $O(n(\log\log n)^{2}/\sqrt{\log n})$. The decisive inequality is $R\ll Q$: many evaluation points with low-degree forms, so that few Taylor coefficients are needed per point. [1]

**The construction (Section 2.7 and Section 4).** This is where the proof earns its result. Index auxiliary coordinates by $\mathcal B=([r]\times[r])^{Q}$, a sequence of $Q$ pairs recording, at each point, the group of a variable and the group of its clause; $D=|\mathcal B|=r^{2Q}$. Let $\Pi_s$ swap the two entries of pair $s$; these commute and square to the identity. Put $V_0=\prod_s\Pi_s$, $W_0=\tfrac12\sum_s(I-\Pi_s)$ and $M_0=V_0f(W_0)$ for a polynomial $f$ of degree at most $R-1$. Expanding, $M_0$ is a combination of products of swaps outside sets $S$ of size at most $R-1$, so an entry $M_0[u,w]$ can be nonzero only if $u$ and $w$ disagree, in the sense that matters, at fewer than $R$ points (Lemma 4.1). The swaps have a common orthogonal eigenbasis of $\pm1$ vectors indexed by the number $j$ of positions carrying eigenvalue $-1$, and $M_0$ multiplies such a vector by $(-1)^{j}f(j)$. Choose $f$ to interpolate $(-1)^{j}$ on an interval of $R$ consecutive integers around the binomial mean $Qp$, $p=(r-1)/2r$; then $M_0$ fixes every basis vector in that interval, and the remaining eigenvectors, at most $2D\exp(-R^{2}/8Q)$ of them by the binomial tail, become the rows of the measurement matrix $C$. The paper traces this interpolation of $(-1)^{j}$ to Alman and Williams's work on matrix rigidity. The replacement for coordinate $u$ is then $((I-M_0)\vec\zeta)_u$, a function of the measurements, plus $\sum_wM_0[u,w]\varphi_{u,w}(T)\zeta_w$ with $\varphi_{u,w}(T)=\prod_{s\in E_{u,w}}(1-T/s)$, a polynomial of degree less than $R$ that is $1$ at $T=0$ and vanishes at exactly the points where $\zeta_w$ sits outside the assigned group (Lemma 4.3). Finally the formula's variables are placed into the auxiliary coordinates by schedules $a_v,d_e\in[r]^{Q}$, chosen deterministically by the method of conditional expectations so that groups are balanced and no label is overused (Lemma 4.5), using $h\le2M/D$ copies of $\mathcal B$ (Section 4.5); the measurement count comes out at most $2\tau n$ (Lemma 4.6), and Lemma 4.8 verifies the parameter inequalities. [1][24]

**From three occurrences to any $k$ (Section 2.8 and Section 6).** The counting-preserving sparsification lemma (Lemma 6.3, Appendix A) splits a $k$-CNF into at most $\mathrm{poly}(n,\sigma^{-1})2^{\sigma n}$ formulas with $B=O_k((\sigma^{-1}\log(2/\sigma))^{k-1})$ occurrences per variable whose solution sets partition the original. OR-gates turn long clauses into 3-clauses, and Tovey's implication cycles replace a variable with $s$ occurrences by $s$ copies forced to agree, each occurring three times. Applied directly this gives $2^{o(n)}$ but a worse exponent, because the copies multiply $n$ by $B$. To keep the exponent, the paper counts instead the common zeros of a system of $O(n)$ sparse polynomial equations over $\mathbb F_2$ on the original variables, each a parity test "the number of violated clauses in this set is even". Lemma 6.1 builds the first $3n$ tests from an expander code, the bipartite double cover of a Cayley multigraph with small-bias shifts, so that any nonsatisfying assignment passing them violates at least $m/P$ clauses; Lemma 6.2 then appends $3n$ further short tests greedily, choosing each to minimise the exact count of survivors (computed by the counting routine itself), which removes at least a quarter of the remaining nonsatisfying assignments each time. To put a parity test into the polynomial framework, the integer count $L_i(x)$ of violated clauses is passed through the complemented Beigel-Tarui polynomial $p_\ell$, which is $1$ modulo $2^{\ell}$ on even integers and $0$ on odd ones, with $\ell=n+1$ (Lemma 5.1); writing $p_\ell$ as $\sum_je_j(y)h(y)^{j}$ with $h(y)=y(y-1)$ and truncating the product across all tests at $\ell$ copies of $h$ keeps the input degree linear in $n$ (Lemma 5.2). Theorem 2.2 and Proposition 5.3 state the resulting counter for sparse polynomial systems; Proposition 6.4 assembles the interpolation algorithm with exponent $O_k(n(\log\log n)^{2}/\sqrt{\log n})$. [1]

**The sharper exponent (Appendix D).** Theorem D.1 replaces the single global Taylor expansion by a recursive contraction of a Boolean tensor network with $O_{k}(n\log\log n)$ wires (or $O_{k,D}(n)$ wires for linear-size formulas), deforming wire identifications so that at an interpolation node only ports in one group interact, expanding interactions between groups in "modes" whose weight-$j$ coefficients are divisible by $2^{j}$, and tapering the Hermite multiplicities by the two-adic valuation of the node (Lemma D.5). This yields the exponents of Theorem 1.1; Appendix B handles exact arithmetic and the space bound. [1]

**Where an expert would look first.** At the design condition and the shift trick together: that freezing $\vec X$ at indicators keeps the group separation intact (Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3). At the count of measurement rows, $c\le D\tau/3r$ from the binomial tail, and the balance lemma that bounds group sizes and label multiplicities (Lemmas 4.5 and 4.6), since the exponent dies if either slips. At the cross-test truncation (Lemma 5.2), which is what keeps the polynomial degree linear when the clause-to-test reduction multiplies factors. And at Appendix D, which carries the sharper exponent and is outside the Lean. Ryan O'Donnell's exposition rewrites the whole argument as computing the permanent of a sparse rational matrix $A(t)$ by interpolation, which gives a reader a second route through the same mechanism. [1][16]

## Consequences inside mathematics

If the theorem holds, ETH, SETH, Gap-ETH, NSETH and $\#$ETH are false, as are their bounded-error randomized versions. The reductions proved under them remain valid theorems; what falls is the conclusion each drew from a now-false premise. The paper is careful about which lower bounds survive on other hypotheses: those for longest common subsequence and regular-expression matching also follow from the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, which the theorem does not touch, and several others follow from the hypothesis that CNF-SAT with unbounded clause width has no $2^{(1-\delta)n}$ algorithm, which again is untouched because the theorem fixes $k$. Where a problem reduces to fixed-width SAT with a linear number of variables, as Set Splitting with bounded sets does, it inherits a subexponential algorithm directly. [1]

Corollary 1.4 turns the algorithm into a lower bound, through Williams's 2010 connection between faster satisfiability algorithms and circuit lower bounds: a language in $\mathrm E^{\mathrm{NP}}$ that needs $n\sqrt{\log n}$ gates. O'Donnell's exposition records Bernardo Subercaseaux's observation that the same method gives $\mathrm{NTIME}(T)\subseteq\mathrm{DTIME}\big(2^{O(T(\log\log T)^{3}/\sqrt{\log T})}\big)$ for multitape Turing machines. On 28 October Impagliazzo and Paturi circulated a five-page note proving that a weaker form of the hypothesis holds for 3-SAT exactly when it holds for $k$-SAT; it does not assess the claim. Within three days of the release, preprints on Hexagon claimed consequences for factoring, for weighted counting and for learning theory, all generated with AI assistance and all resting on the claim as a black box; a spreadsheet of cryptographic consequences is being collected on the examiners' blog. None of these has been checked. [1][9][13][15][16][23][25]

## Uses outside mathematics

No known direct application. The claim is about asymptotic growth with constants the paper does not state, and by our reading of its parameter choices the bound applies only to formulas far larger than any that could be stored. OpenAI's own announcement says that finding practical algorithms and "turning theoretical advances into practical improvements, will require significant further research". [1][11]

The indirect effect is real but negative in form. ETH and SETH were used, in OpenAI's words, "in hundreds of papers to derive conditional computational hardness results across a wide variety of fields including graph theory, computational biology, databases, computational geometry, and more". Those papers proved that certain problems are hard if ETH holds; the refutation removes the premise, not the problems, and the industrial SAT solvers used in hardware verification and planning are untouched at every size they handle. Williams wrote that one of his first requests to OpenAI was to check that no existing cryptosystem is compromised by the result, and that "apparently" none is; we record that as his report of OpenAI's answer, not as an independent finding. [11][23]

## Has anyone checked it?

OpenAI did not release this result the way it released the October 6 collection. It approached outside experts in advance and, following their advice, formed "an external group of collaborators to explain the result, while making the AI output publicly available for anyone to examine and build on". The group is led by Impagliazzo and Paturi of UC San Diego, who proposed ETH, and Williams of MIT. OpenAI's statement says the collaboration is unpaid, without non-disclosure agreements, that members "do not speak on behalf of OpenAI", and that OpenAI agreed to the group's two requests: to make "all raw data of the results" public, and to fund a conference. [11]

The three organizers published an open letter the same day. Its second paragraph reads: "Our involvement should not be taken as certification of the claim. A result of this significance requires careful, independent scrutiny and an understanding that others can reconstruct, question, and build upon." They set up a blog, a shared repository of notes, a feedback form and a proposed virtual meeting before the end of 2026, and asked the community to "examine the argument, establish its correctness or identify its limitations, and understand its consequences". Williams added on Bluesky that they had "signed no NDAs and will receive no compensation", that they asked OpenAI to "publicly release ASAP all their raw data regarding ETH, including PDFs and Lean source", and, asked whether they could question the model, that "We do not have access to their internal models. We only got the PDF." As of 1 November the examiners have posted no finding on the claim itself. [12][13][14]

Others have worked through the argument in public, each saying what they have not done. Ryan O'Donnell of Carnegie Mellon posted an exposition that reorganises the proof around sparse permanents, revised through thirteen drafts between 26 and 30 October and recorded as a lecture; its footnote says it was "Produced ... through interaction with LLMs". Shiva Kintali posted "A simplified proof of ETH is false" on 26 October, proving the qualitative $2^{\epsilon n}$ statement by the paper's method with simpler parameters. Mark Sellke posted slides outlining the algorithm. Lance Fortnow, a complexity theorist at Illinois Tech, wrote on 28 October: "I have not verified the proof myself, but it is safe to assume now that the exponential-time hypothesis is toast." These are expositions and judgments, not reports of a completed check, and this site does not count them as approval. [15][16][17][18]

Two things distinguish this claim from the rest of the release. First, it is not alone: Anthropic published "A $2^{o(n)}$ Time Algorithm for 3-SAT", credited to an internal Claude model, hours after OpenAI's announcement, with the same headline exponent, its own Lean formalization on Mathlib's Turing machines, and a README stating that "The result is purely asymptotic and says nothing about solving formulas in practice." Two independent machine-found proofs of the same new theorem, each machine-checked against its own statement, is a situation without precedent; it is still not a human check of either. Second, the examination has a public record. The examiners' blog carries, besides the letter, a list of expositions, a thread of Lean replications, and from 1 November a call for results that follow from the claim. [12][13][19][20]

No named expert has said publicly that the proof holds. None has reported an error. The label is "under review", which this site uses when named experts are reading a proof and have given no verdict. [12][15]

**Machine checking.** The release's catalogue `formalization.yaml` lists a Lean statement for this paper, `SubexponentialSAT.lean`, 220 lines, with a formal proof of 3,977 files. The statement defines its own model of computation, a machine with several tapes, and asserts one fixed machine that, for every $k$-CNF on $n$ variables, halts with the exact count on one tape, with the satisfiability bit on another, within time $C(L+1)^{d}2^{A\cdot n(\log\log(n+16))^{2}/\sqrt{\log(n+16)}}$ for existentially quantified constants, where $L$ is the input's bit length, having visited at most $S(L+1)^{e}$ tape cells. That is the weaker exponent of the paper's Section 6, not the one in Theorem 1.1; the scope note says so. The statement does not define ETH. Carlos Toledo replayed the development under Lean's kernel and the independent checker nanoda, wrote a textbook ETH in Lean, and proved that the statement refutes it within the statement's own machine model, while noting that whether that model matches the one in the hypothesis "is a reading and always will be"; his conclusion is that the replay "does not certify that ETH is false". Totó Busnello reported a second replay on the examiners' blog: 3 hours 47 minutes over 12,014 build jobs, with the kernel accepting the proof under the three standard axioms, and a hand reading of the statement in which one check, the cost of the unary input encoding, was left open. A kernel check is evidence about the formal statement, not a reading of the paper; Uruk does not count it as approval. [13][21][22]

The pinned commit is the repository as it stood on 29 October 2026, three days after this family was added. Its catalogue counts 721 manuscripts in 373 families; this paper is the only one in family 378, and the only update to the repository since it appeared concerns a different family. [1][23]
